Self-employment tax explained: what the 15.3% really pays for

Self-employment tax is often the biggest tax a new freelancer pays. Here’s what it is, how it’s worked out, and the legal ways to lower it.

By taxmetter team October 17, 2026 2 min read

In this article

  2. How it’s calculated
  3. The Social Security cap
  4. The deduction that helps
  5. Ways to lower it

New freelancers are often surprised that their tax bill is bigger than it was as an employee, even on the same income. The reason is self-employment tax.

What it is

Every worker pays into Social Security and Medicare. As an employee you pay 7.65% and your employer quietly pays another 7.65%. When you work for yourself, you’re both the employee and the employer, so you pay both halves: 15.3%.

  • 12.4% goes to Social Security
  • 2.9% goes to Medicare

How it’s calculated

The tax isn’t charged on your whole profit. First, multiply your net profit by 92.35%. This mirrors the fact that employees don’t pay tax on their employer’s share. Then apply 15.3% to the result.

For $50,000 of profit: $50,000 × 92.35% = $46,175, and $46,175 × 15.3% = $7,065.

If your net earnings from self-employment are under $400 for the year, you don’t owe self-employment tax at all.

The Social Security cap

The 12.4% Social Security part only applies up to the wage base, which is $184,500 in 2026. Above that, only the 2.9% Medicare part continues.

If you also have a W-2 job, your wages count towards that cap first. A high earner with a day job may pay little or no Social Security tax on side income.

At higher incomes there’s also an Additional Medicare tax of 0.9% on earnings above $200,000 for single filers ($250,000 married filing jointly).

The deduction that helps

You can deduct half of your self-employment tax from your income before income tax is worked out. It doesn’t lower the self-employment tax itself, but it does lower your income tax.

Ways to lower it

  • Claim every business expense. Self-employment tax is charged on profit, so expenses lower it directly. See our list of deductions freelancers often miss.
  • Keep good records. Mileage logs and receipts turn into real savings.
  • Know what doesn’t help. Retirement contributions and the standard deduction lower income tax, but not self-employment tax.
