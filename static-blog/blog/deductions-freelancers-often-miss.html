9 tax deductions freelancers often miss

Every legitimate business expense lowers both your income tax and your self-employment tax. These are the ones that most often get left off.

As a 1099 worker, a business expense saves you more than it would an employee, because it lowers your self-employment tax as well as your income tax. On $60,000 of freelance income, each $1,000 of expenses saves about $230, and more at higher incomes.

The rule is simple: an expense must be ordinary and necessary for your work. Here are nine that are easy to forget.

1. Business mileage

Driving to clients, job sites, the bank or the post office for work all count. For 2026 the standard rate is 72.5¢ a mile from January to June and 76¢ from July. Keep a log with the date, purpose and miles. Commuting to a regular workplace doesn’t count.

2. Home office

If you use part of your home regularly and only for work, the simplified method gives $5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet. Read more in our home office guide.

3. Phone and internet

The business share of your phone and home internet bills is deductible. If you use them half for work, deduct half.

4. Software and subscriptions

Accounting software, design tools, cloud storage, website hosting, your domain name and paid professional apps all count.

5. Equipment

A laptop, monitor, camera or tools you buy for work can usually be deducted in the year you buy them.

6. Health insurance premiums

If you pay for your own health insurance and can’t join an employer plan (yours or your spouse’s), the premiums are deductible from your income.

7. Retirement contributions

Money you put into a SEP-IRA or solo 401(k) lowers your income tax and builds your savings at the same time.

8. Fees and commissions

Platform fees from marketplaces and payment processors are a business expense. Check your 1099-K: it often shows the gross amount before fees were taken.

9. Learning and professional costs

Courses that improve skills for your current work, industry books, professional memberships and the cost of a tax professional to prepare your business return.

Keep the paperwork

Save receipts and bank statements for at least three years. A separate bank account for your business makes this much easier.
