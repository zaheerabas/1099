Quarterly estimated taxes for 2026: due dates and how much to pay

If you expect to owe $1,000 or more, the IRS wants tax four times a year, not once in April. Here are the 2026 dates and the rules that keep you penalty-free.

The US tax system is pay-as-you-go. Employees pay through withholding every paycheck. If you’re self-employed, you do the same thing yourself with estimated tax payments.

Who needs to pay

You generally need to make estimated payments if you expect to owe $1,000 or more in federal tax for the year after subtracting any withholding. That’s most freelancers with a few thousand dollars of profit or more.

The 2026 due dates

  • Q1: April 15, 2026 (income from January to March)
  • Q2: June 15, 2026 (April and May)
  • Q3: September 15, 2026 (June to August)
  • Q4: January 15, 2027 (September to December)

The periods aren’t equal quarters, which catches people out. Q2 covers only two months but is due just two months after Q1.

How much to pay

The simplest approach is to estimate your total tax for the year and divide it by four. If your income is uneven, you can pay more in the quarters you earn more.

If you start late in the year, spread what you still owe over the due dates that are left. The calculator does this automatically and highlights your next payment.

Avoiding the underpayment penalty

You won’t owe a penalty if, through withholding and estimated payments, you pay at least the smaller of:

  • 90% of your 2026 tax, or
  • 100% of your 2025 tax (110% if your 2025 adjusted gross income was over $150,000)

The second option is useful when your income is growing: you can base your payments on last year’s known bill and settle the rest in April without a penalty.

How to pay

You can pay online through IRS Direct Pay or your IRS online account, through the Electronic Federal Tax Payment System (EFTPS), or by mailing a check with a Form 1040-ES voucher. Online payments give you an instant confirmation, which is worth keeping.

If you miss one

Pay as soon as you can. The penalty works like interest, growing with the amount and the number of days you’re late, so a payment a few weeks late costs far less than skipping it.
